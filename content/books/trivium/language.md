section
  title The Function of Language
  num 01
  subtitle Language has a threefold function: to communicate thought, volition, and emotion.

  para
    Only humans can unite sounds in a sentence to [em]express thought,[/em] because humans alone are rational (have something to say), social (have someone to say it to), and corporeal (require a physical medium). Animals communicate emotion through interjections — cries that cannot be assimilated into a sentence. Pure spirits communicate thought without a physical medium; their communication is not properly called language.

  grid 3
    card
      step Rational
      Humans have something to say — they can apprehend and express essence.
    card
      step Social
      Humans have someone to say it to — they exist in community.
    card
      step Corporeal
      Humans require a physical medium — articulate voice, or its written substitute.

section
  title Means of Communication
  num 02
  subtitle Two and only two modes of communicating ideas through a physical medium.

  grid 2
    card
      step Imitation
      An artificial likeness: painting, photograph, pantomime, gesture, picture writing. Meaning is unmistakable — it means what it resembles. Limited: cannot express the [em]essences[/em] of things.
    card
      step Symbol
      An arbitrary sensible sign whose meaning is imposed by convention. Signs may derive meaning from [em]nature[/em] (cloud → rain) or [em]convention[/em] (green light → go). Convention-symbols may be temporary or permanent; [em]special[/em] (expert-designed, precise, international) or [em]common[/em] (invented by common people — more adequate but more ambiguous).

  para
    Common language may be native or foreign, living or dead, spoken (the original form) or written (the most important substitute).

section
  title The Nature of Language
  num 03
  subtitle A word, like every physical reality, is constituted of matter and form.

  para
    Language is a [em]system of symbols[/em] for expressing thoughts, volitions, and emotions. A word has matter — the physical sound or mark — and form — its meaning. The matter of spoken language is studied by [em]phonetics;[/em] the matter of written language by [em]orthography;[/em] meaning by [em]semantics.[/em]

  grid 3
    card
      step Matter — Spoken
      Sound. The human voice is symbolic — its meaning is imposed by convention, not nature. Articulate voice modified by tongue, palate, teeth, and lips produces 20 vowel sounds, 6 diphthongs, and 27 consonant sounds.
    card
      step Matter — Written
      Mark or notation. The most important substitute for the spoken word, enabling communication across time and distance.
    card
      step Form
      Meaning. Words symbolize both [em]individuals[/em] (any physical being unique by reason of matter) and [em]essences[/em] (that which makes a being what it is, shared by a class).

  grid 2
    card
      step Species
      Class of individuals sharing the same specific essence.
    card
      step Genus
      Wider class embracing two or more different species.
    card
      step Individual
      Any physical being that exists; unique by reason of matter.
    card
      step Aggregate
      A particular group of individuals — not a species or genus; does not include all members of a nature.

section
  title Language and Its Symbols
  num 04
  subtitle Four kinds of symbol — two for individuals, two for essences.

  grid 2
    card
      step Proper Name
      Refers to an individual or aggregate. Without proper names: no history, no literature, no emergency directions.
    card
      step Particular Description
      An empirical description picking out an individual or aggregate — indirectly but uniquely.
    card
      step Common Name
      Represents an essence — a species or genus. Without common names: no universal ideas, no science, no philosophy.
    card
      step General Description
      A universal description of an essence; defines rather than merely names.

  important 
    color red
    label Empty Words
    Words representing no reality are [em]empty words[/em] — e.g., [i]"present King of France."[/i] A common name or general description must represent an intrinsically possible essence to be meaningful: [i]mermaid[/i] has meaning; [i]square circle[/i] does not.

section
  title Creating Symbols from Reality
  num 05
  subtitle From sensation to concept — how the intellect abstracts essence from experience.

  grid 3
    card
      step Percept
      Sense-apprehension of an individual reality [i]in its presence.[/i] The starting point of all knowledge.
    card
      step Phantasm
      Mental image of an individual reality [i]in its absence.[/i] Formed by four internal senses: imagination, sensuous memory, common sense, and instinct.
    card
      step Concept
      Intellectual apprehension of essence — universal and immaterial. Only the intellect abstracts essence from phantasms. Animals have keener senses but lack this power; hence no progress, no culture.

  note
    Abstract knowledge is [em]clearer but less vivid[/em] than sense knowledge. Words are symbols of phantasms and concepts — not of the things themselves.

subsection
  title The Ten Categories of Being
  subtitle Aristotle's praedicamenta — the ten fundamental modes of predication.

  list
    [i]Substance[/i] — exists in itself (e.g., man)
    [i]Quantity[/i] — determination of matter (e.g., tall)
    [i]Quality[/i] — determination of form (e.g., intelligent)
    [i]Relation[/i] — reference to another (e.g., friend)
    [i]Action[/i] — exercise of a power producing an effect (e.g., clicking)
    [i]Passion[/i] — reception of an effect (e.g., being drafted)
    [i]When[/i] — position relative to time (e.g., Sunday afternoon)
    [i]Where[/i] — position relative to surrounding bodies (e.g., beside the lake)
    [i]Posture[/i] — relative position of parts (e.g., sitting)
    [i]Habiliment[/i] — clothing or ornament (e.g., in gray tweeds)

  para
    Three subcategories by what the predicate expresses: (1) the subject itself; (2) something existing in the subject; (3) something extrinsic to the subject.

  para
    A [em]construct[/em] is a composite concept including essential accidents — e.g., [i]lawyer, carpenter, blizzard.[/i] It can be analyzed into its categorical components: substance + quality + action + relation, etc.

section
  title Logical and Psychological Dimensions
  num 06
  subtitle Every word carries both a logical (denotative) and a psychological (connotative) charge.

  grid 2
    card
      step Logical Dimension
      title Denotation
      The thought content of a word; expressible in its dictionary definition. Desirable in legal, scientific, and philosophical writing — synonyms to be avoided, abstract words preferred. To teach abstract knowledge, use concrete illustrations so the reader forms the abstraction himself.
    card
      step Psychological Dimension
      title Connotation
      The emotional content: related images, nuances, emotion spontaneously associated with a word. Desirable in poetry and literature — concrete, imagistic, idiomatic words preferred. Translation succeeds in the logical dimension but fails in the psychological.

subsection
  title Sub-Topics of the Psychological Dimension
  subtitle Sound, style, idiom, allusion, word combination, and the poetic use of falsehood.

  grid 3
    card
      step Sound
      Mere sound produces psychological effects independent of meaning — as in de la Mare's "Silver."
    card
      step Pedantic Style
      Pompous diction is psychologically displeasing even when logically equivalent. The choice of words matters beyond their definitions.
    card
      step Idiom
      Emotional effect is a by-product of historical development — lost in translation. Compare [i]swine[/i] (farm) with [i]pork[/i] (table): same referent, different resonance.
    card
      step Allusion
      A passing reference to shared literary or cultural heritage enriches the psychological dimension without burdening the logical.
    card
      step Combination of Words
      Adjective-noun and verb-noun pairings can be "just right" or disharmonious — [i]azure apron[/i] clashes where [i]azure sky[/i] harmonizes.
    card
      step Logical vs. Poetic
      What is false literally may be true figuratively. Metaphor operates in the psychological dimension — as in Donne's "Song."

section
  title The Ambiguity of Language
  num 07
  subtitle Words are inherently ambiguous — their meaning is imposed by convention, not nature.

  para
    Three sources of ambiguity arise from the nature of language itself.

  grid 3
    card
      step Source A
      title History of Words
      A word acquires multiple meanings over time, producing homonyms. The relationship may be [em]equivocal[/em] (nothing in common: [i]sound[/i] heard / [i]sound[/i] a body of water) or [em]analogical[/em] (something shared: [i]march[/i] the step / [i]march[/i] the music). The dictionary records usage; it does not legislate.
    card
      step Source B
      title Nature of the Phantasm
      Ambiguity arising from the mental image a word originally stood for. The phantasm is individual and concrete; the concept is universal. Words hover between the two.
    card
      step Source C
      title Deliberate Ambiguity
      Puns and irony exploit ambiguity for effect. [em]Dead metaphor[/em] — a figure whose figurative origin has been forgotten and is now treated as literal — reveals how the psychological dimension fossilizes into the logical over time.

subsection
  title Imposition and Intention
  subtitle The three ways a word may refer to itself or to the world — and the two ways a term intends its object.

  grid 3
    card
      step Zero Imposition
      Refers to the word as a sensible sign only — to its sound or notation, without reference to meaning. The basis of phonetics, spelling, and conundrums.
    card
      step First Imposition
      Ordinary predicative use — refers to the reality it symbolizes. The window you see [i]through,[/i] not [i]at.[/i]
    card
      step Second Imposition
      Refers to the word as a grammatical sign with meaning. Grammar is the science of second impositions.

  grid 2
    card
      step First Intention
      A term refers to a real thing — a physical entity.
    card
      step Second Intention
      A term refers to a logical abstraction — a class, a species, a concept. Logic is the science of second intentions.
